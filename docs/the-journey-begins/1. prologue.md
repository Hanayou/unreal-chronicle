## 🖼️ How Games Exist — The Game Development Pipeline

Many new developers open a game engine, drop in an asset, and make it move—without really understanding *what* it is.  
Let’s take a moment to explain how a character, a prop, or a scene actually exists inside a game engine.  
Think of this not as a full “studio pipeline” guide, but as **context** so you can understand what UE5 is working with under the hood.

### 1. **Meshes — The Shapes of the World**
(*[Image placeholder: simple cube mesh + complex character mesh]*)

At the heart of every 3D asset is **geometry**:
- **Vertices**: points in 3D space
- **Edges**: lines connecting vertices
- **Faces**: flat surfaces made from edges (most often triangles)

These combine into **meshes**—the raw shapes that make up everything from pebbles to player characters.  
UE5 stores these as Static Meshes (non-moving) or Skeletal Meshes (animated).

### 2. **UV Mapping — Flattening the 3D World**
(*[Image placeholder: sphere → UV unwrapped grid]*)

A mesh’s surface is “unwrapped” into 2D coordinates called **UVs**, so a flat texture can be wrapped correctly around it.  
Bad UVs mean stretched or misaligned textures.

### 3. **Texturing — The Illusion of Detail**
(*[Image placeholder: color map, normal map, roughness map side-by-side]*)

Textures are images applied to the UVs to define how a surface looks:
- **Base Color / Albedo** – the main surface colour
- **Normal Map** – adds the illusion of depth and small details without extra geometry
- **Roughness / Metallic Maps** – control how shiny or matte a surface is
- **Ambient Occlusion** – adds baked-in shadow depth

These are often packed together in performance-friendly ways.

### 4. **Rigging & Skinning — Bringing Meshes to Life**
(*[Image placeholder: character skeleton overlayed on mesh]*)

Animated objects, especially characters, have a skeleton (**rig**).  
Each mesh vertex is assigned weights telling it how much it follows each bone (**skinning**). Without this, your character couldn’t bend an elbow.

### 5. **Animation — Motion in Action**
(*[Image placeholder: animation keyframes or mocap capture]*)

Animations can be hand-keyed or motion-captured. In UE5, these are blended and controlled through the **Animation Blueprint** system.

### 6. **Extra Components — Beyond the Basics**
(*[Image placeholder: particle system, sound wave, light icon]*)

Game scenes use more than just meshes and textures:
- **Particle Systems** (smoke, sparks, magic effects)
- **Lighting** (dynamic, baked, global illumination)
- **Audio** (sound cues, 3D spatialisation)
- **Materials & Shaders** (mathematical surface effects)

### 7. **Inside the Engine — Where It All Comes Together**
The engine assembles these into your world. It also:
- Manages **draw calls** (instructions to the GPU)
- Streams assets in/out as needed
- Runs physics, AI, and gameplay logic

💡 Even at this stage, choices you make affect performance—number of meshes, texture resolution, lighting method—so we’ll build with an eye for scalability from the start.

## 🧭 General Guidance for Your Journey

### 1. Start Rough
In well-architected systems, you can swap placeholders for final assets without rewriting gameplay.  
Don’t model the perfect sword before you know your combat system works—use a cube, and replace it later. Imagine spending weeks or even months on art assets up front, only to find out the game isn't fun and you need to scrap/rework all your assets for a new idea.

Overwatch is a fantastic example of this done right, where 4-5 months of it's earliest playtests (almost half of it's full ~1 year dev cycle until beta release) used placeholder assets, and broken animations to validate the gameplay first.

### 2. “Premature Optimization is the Root of All Evil”
The saying is usually taken to mean: **don’t waste time optimising something if performance isn't yet an issue** — especially as the implemtation might change during development, meaning optimisations need to be rewritten too.  
However, this statement is often taken to the extreme and some obvious optimisations ***should*** be considered early. In game development, examples such as:
- Avoid massive texture sizes when a smaller atlas would work
- Be mindful of draw calls—merging meshes and reusing materials where logical
- Don’t run expensive calculations every frame (UI redraws, asset streaming)

If you don't know what these are yet, don't worry - we'll talk more about them (and many more) as we go! Keeping these optimisations in mind early on as you develop your game and assets can help alleviate optimisation nightmares weeks or months into development.

In this section, I’ll show you **how to work smart from the start**—without getting trapped in endless micro-optimisations.

### 3. There’s No One True Way — But Every Way Has Trade-offs
There's very rarely one way to achieve something. One thing might uniquely work for your specific game or problem that no one else needs. Keep an open mind on how to approach problems, but don't let me anyone tell you that there's only one 'right' way to solve a problem. Examples of trade-offs happen at all levels of game development:

- **Asset Naming Conventions**  
  Ensuring assets have clear, consistent names take more effort but make teamwork (and future-you) much happier. `SM_Table_Round` is better than `testtable02`.
  
- **Lighting Choices**  
  Dynamic lights give flexibility, baked lights give performance. Which you choose depends on the game’s needs and target hardware.

- **Gameplay Implementation**  
  A mechanic might be quick to implement in Blueprints, but better long-term in C++ for performance. Sometimes you start in BP, then migrate later.

> Good development is about knowing when and where to make trade-offs.
